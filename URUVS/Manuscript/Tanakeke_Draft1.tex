\section{ABSTRACT}\label{abstract}

Mangroves are an essential biome for many tropical ecosystems and coastal communities. They exhibit high carbon sequestration, protect coastal areas from floods and extreme weather events, and foster biodiversity in their habitats. They have also been shown to increase fish stocks in surrounding fisheries. As the country with the most extensive mangrove habitats in the world, the Indonesian government has committed to restoring 600,000 hectars of mangroves throughout the country. One such place where this restoration effort is taking place is Pulau Tanakeke in South Sulawesi. Here, an NGO called Blue Forests has been conducing mangrove restoration since 2012. In the project, we use Unbaited Remote Underwater Video to assess how these replanting efforts are effecting local fish stocks and biodiversity. We compare three types of mangrove forests: natural (old-growth forests), EMR (replanted mangroves), and areas with destroyed mangroves. While we were unable to demonstrate a clear statistical relationship between our sampling sites in regards to fish diversity and abundance, this project serves as a baseline survey of the fish groups that live in these ecosystems in South Sulawesi. The benefits of mangroves for small scale fisheries is multi-dimensional and this research highlights the importance of environmental restoration for community engagement and ensuring food security.

\section{INTRODUCTION}\label{introduction}

Mangroves are inter-tidal forests that are essential components to many tropical ecosystems. As the effects of climate change grow stronger worldwide, the need for carbon mitigation and protection against extreme weather are becoming more urgent. These biomes comprise about 14 \% of marine carbon sequestration and may result in high gas emissions when these ecosystems are disturbed (Daniel M. Alongi 2012), while more established mangroves are more efficient in absorbing atmospheric carbon (Cameron, Hutley, and Friess 2019). Beyond their benefits of protecting against extreme weather events, mangroves are also key actors in maintaining the biodiversity of the ecosystems they inhabit. Mangroves have been reported to support up 20\% of the benthic biodiversity in their habitats (Carugati et al. 2018). They provide essential nutrients, temperature controls, and protection from predators for marine life (Blue Forests 2012). Further, Mangroves have been shown to increase fishery yields in their surrounding areas, therefore increasing fisher income (Aburto-Oropeza et al. 2008). The root systems of mangroves provide shelter and protection for juvenile fish, allowing them to grow and develop safely away from predators and also act as a buffer against strong currents and waves, creating calmer and more stable environments where fish can feed and reproduce (Daniel M. Alongi 2008). Areas with intact mangrove forests have been shown to support higher fish abundance and diversity compared to areas without mangroves (I. Nagelkerken et al. 2008). Mangroves provide a rich food web, with leaf litter and detritus serving as a source of nutrients that fuel the basis of the food chain, supporting the growth and survival of various fish species (Daniel M. Alongi 2008). Furthermore, mangroves act as a buffer against coastal erosion and storm surges, safeguarding the habitats of both fish and fishermen (I. Nagelkerken et al. 2008). Mangroves offer a crucial line of defense against the impacts of climate change on fisheries. The dense root systems of mangroves stabilize shorelines and protect coastal areas from erosion caused by rising sea levels and extreme weather events (Daniel M. Alongi 2008). As the largest archipelago in the world, marine fisheries are an extremely important resource in Indonesia for food security and fisher income. Fisheries contribute to about 3\% of the GDP in Indonesia, and over 80\% of fishery catches are from small scale fisheries, however these systems are currently subject to over-exploitation, threatening the food security of those who are reliant on these fisheries (Ayunda, Sapota, and Pawelec 2018).

Despite all of their contributions to ecosystem health, mangrove environments are being threatened worldwide. Rising sea-levels has been shown to be a major contributor to mangrove loss (Gilman et al. 2008). Further, as extreme events are becoming more intense and more frequent, these could potentially threaten mangroves due to defoliation, soil erosion, or by altering the chemical makeup or temperature of soils (Gilman et al. 2008). Mangroves are also directly threatened by anthropogenic activity. Pollution, coastal development, and aquaculture development have also contributed to mangrove ecosystem loss (Adeel and Pomeroy 2002). Mangrove forests in the Western Tropical Pacific are the most diverse of these habitats globally (Ellison, Farnsworth, and Merkt 1999). Indonesia has the most extensive mangrove forests in the world (Kusmana 2011). As Indonesia is also the largest archipelagic country, mangroves' contribution to flood protection and extreme weather mitigation is vital to the health and safety of many of its inhabitants. However, due to timber production, aquaculture, and human development, the Ministry of Forestry reported in 2007 that around 69\% of mangroves were in damaged condition in the country (Kusmana 2011). As a response to this habitat loss, the Indonesian government has committed to restoring 600,000 hectares of mangroves in the country in 2024, the most ambitious mangrove restoration project in the world.

Tanakeke Island in South Sulawesi, Takalar Regency, is one location where this restoration effort is taking place. Here, mangrove habitats are generally privately owned, and the main driver of mangrove loss has been timber production and shrimp aquaculture (Blue Forests 2012). 1,200 hectares of mangroves were converted into aquaculture ponds in the 1980s and 1990s, which is about 70\% of its historical cover (Brown et al. 2014). Recently, there have been reports of increased flooding on this island which could be attributed to the loss of protection from mangroves (Blue Forests 2012). However, in response to mangrove restoration, the community has seen great success in both engagement and successful mangrove rehabilitation, where hundreds of community members have participated since the 1990's (Blue Forests 2012; {``Past and {Present} on {Tanakeke Island}''} 2013). Further, small scale fishing is an essential part of peoples' livelihoods on Tanakeke. Locally caught seafood is the main source of protein on this island (Blue Forests 2012). Mangroves provide refuge for about 55\% fish catch biomass in Indonesia (World Bank 2022). Fisher income has been shown to be negatively affected by mangrove habitat loss, and because of this, the financial gain of mangrove restoration is predicted to be more lucrative than any alternative land use such as aquaculture (Yamamoto 2023). For this reason, mangroves are an essential component to the health of Indonesia's fisheries and contribute significantly to the food security of those directly reliant on small scale fisheries as a key source of nutrition, including on Tanakeke Island (Blue Forests 2012). While these mangrove restoration efforts have resulted in increased flood prevention and ecotourism, little research has been done to assess the marine biodiversity and benefits to fish stocks. In this project, we aim to understand how this restoration is affecting local marine biodiversity and abundance of fish species on Tanakeke Island. We will use Unbaited Remote Underwater Video (URUV) methods to assess the marine biodiversity of mangrove habitats and compare them to the biodiversity of areas that have not undergone mangrove rehabilitation.

\section{METHODS}\label{methods}

\subsection{Site description:}\label{site-description}

Pulau Tanakeke (Figure \ref{fig:Tanakeke}) is situated about 40 km southwest of Makassar in South Sulawesi, Takalar Regency. The island is a coral atoll covering about 3,930 hectares. About 392.25 hectares of mangrove has been restored (Cameron, Hutley, and Friess 2019). Access to the island presents a challenge as rough seas make sea crossings difficult during rainy seasons. The island comprises of six villages or desa: Mattirobaji, Lantang Peo, Tompotana, Maccinibaji, Balangdatu, and Rewataya. Historically, the island was populated with about 1,776 hectares of Mangroves, most of which has been destroyed for aquaculture.

\subsection{URUVs}\label{uruvs}

The study was conducted on Tanakeke Island in the Takalar Regency of South Sulawesi, Indonesia in the villages of Tompotanah and Lantangpeo (Fig PETA\_AIS). In each village, mangroves were classified into three type: degraded, natural and EMR. EMR (Ecological Mangrove Restoration) refers to a low-cost, socially focused mangrove restoration method used by Blue Forests where local communities are involved in every step of the restoration process, and various mangrove replanting techniques are applied to the restoration site (Brown et al. 2014). Three drop sites were selected in each mangrove type in each village based on mangrove type, water depth, and permission from land owners. Further, cameras were placed next to vegetation because this reduced the likelihood of boat traffic knocking over camera stands.

URUV methods followed standard BRUV methods outlined in (Langlois et al. 2020) but bait was not used. GoPro SJCAM SJ4000 Action Camera 4K30fps WiFi Cameras were installed on a 1 meter-tall PVC pipe partially buried in the sediment. Two villages were sampled and three cameras were installed in each village in each mangrove type, a total of 18 sites (PETA\_AIS). The cameras were deployed horizontally to the ground with an additional 1 m PVC pipe extending parallel to the ground below the camera so that the length of that pipe was visible in the video. Tick marks spaced 10 cm apart were painted onto the pipe visible in the video. This allowed for a standardization of water visibility, as the number of ticks was counted for each video vile. Because this region experiences a wide tidal range, with mangrove forests being fully exposed to air at low tide, camera depths varied as they were deployed at high tide between the hours of 06:00 and 18:00 to ensure sufficient sunlight and that the camera stayed fully submerged during the duration of filming. Cameras faced vegetation in all sites. Site locations were chosen based on low-boat traffic and consent of the landowner. Date, time of day, tide height, water height at location, weather, and water temperature were recorded at each data collection.

\subsection{Video Analysis}\label{video-analysis}

Camera calibrations were conducted by counting the number of tick marks visible on the extending PVC pipe. Videos were then cut to approximately one hour, starting from five minutes after the camera was set. Videos were watched manually on Windows Media Player and MaxN and T1st were counted and recorded for each fish species found on the video. Fish were identified down the lowest possible taxonomic level and crabs were identified at the family-level. Gastropods were not counted as video-clarity was not sufficient to get accurate counts. A sub-sample of images were cross checked in order to avoid misidentified fish species, and species were identified using CITE DROPBOX BOOKS, BOOK ON SHELF. Further, we gathered input from members from both villages, Tompotanah and Lantangpeo, to collect locally-used names in the Makassar Language.

After video data was analyzed and fish species were identified and counted, data was analyzed using RStudio software (Posit team 2024). At each village and mangrove type, total fish, total species of fish, Shannon's Diversity Index and Simpson's Diversity Index (Magurran 1988) was calculated. We then analyzed trends over time, as well as differences between the mangrove types with regards to fish diversity and assemblages. Finally, trophic scores were tabulated using information from FishBase (Boettiger, Temple Lang, and Wainwright 2012) to determine if different mangrover types were supporting different components of marine food chains.

In total, we identified 41 different animal groups, with 24 fish groups, 1 species of snake, 2 genuses of crab, and various species of shrimp (which were grouped together due to difficulty of identification). Of these, we were able to identify 13 groups to the species level, 6 genuses, and 1 groups to the family level. After groups were identified down to their lowest taxonomic level, we asked fishers of both villages for the local name used for these fish (Table \ref{tab:speciesFound}).

Viewing the data in a time series, both diversity indices showed a low point in June, whereas the total MaxN for that month was among the highest (Figure \ref{fig:Timeseries}). This could indicate that June is a month with high fish counts, but may be dominated by only a few species.

\section{DISCUSSION}\label{discussion}

Zu Ermgassen et al. (2025) has estimated that mangroves support an annual abundance of over 700 billion juvenile fish and invertebrates, and this study only incorporated targeted species. Although our findings didn't demonstrate a clear statistical relationship between habitat type and fish abundance or biodiversity, we were able to characterize the fish species that inhabit these regions, as well as which species were of interest to the local community. Further, as June was a month with high fishcounts yet low diversity, this could indicate a possible breeding season as most of these fish were either cardinal fish, or unidentifiable juveniles. The benefits of mangroves to juvenile fish are known but hard to quantify. Large-scale studies have demonstrated juvenile fish prefer mangrove habitats, even over other ares such as mudflats or seagrass {[}Laegdsgaard and Johnson (1995); Ivan Nagelkerken (2009); zuermgassenMangrovesSupportEstimated2025{]}. Laegdsgaard and Johnson (1995) also found that most of these juveniles are actually not permanent residents of mangroves, residing there primarily in summer months and moving on to other habitats after a summer growth period in mangroves. This is consistent with our findings, where the spike in fish counts in June could be attributed to large counts of juvenile fish. However, habitat preference is influenced by many factors, and growing research suggests that the preference for structural complexity versus the shade benefits of mangroves is different depending on the fish species (Cocheret De La Morinière et al. 2004; Igulu et al. 2014). This could possibly explain why higher counts of fish were recorded at EMR sites vs the natural or destroyed mangroves; tidal patterns, shade, and benthic and water chemistry may also be factors in fish habitat preference. Mangrove destruction and fragmentation has been demonstrated to have negative impacts specifically on juvenile assemblages (Zu Ermgassen et al. 2025). However, although video data may be the best method for studying juvenile assemblages, identifying juveniles from video analysis alone is difficult and certainly a challenge in this study. Stereo-BRUVS or stereo-URUVS, which are video techniques that set two, inward facing cameras to get size estimations of fish, offer one potential opportunity to better study these assemblages (Piggott et al. 2020).

EMR sites showed slightly different dynamics from both the destroyed and natural sites, indicating these areas may be in a temporary imbalance, as most fish species actually preferred these sites, and these areas had a higher proportion of fish with higher trophic scores. As mangroves regrow and the local ecosystem transitions from open water with sandy and rocky benthos to vegitation and organic sediments, the local fauna may be in transition as well. This is especially important on Tanakeke, where substrate contents, tidal patterns, and natural lack of mangrove diversity actually make restoration particularly challenging (Forests 2012; Cameron, Hutley, and Friess 2019; Kusumaningtyas et al. 2022). This can influence not only the efficacy of restoration efforts, but also the ecosystem function of mangroves as they regrow (Cameron et al. 2019).

Although we were unable to show statistical significance in the difference in fish biodiversity among different mangrove sites on Tanakeke, the benefits of mangroves to local communities and local biodiversity is a well supported topic of research and certainly applies to Tanakeke as the community has dedicated itself to restoring these forests to increase protection from storms, rejuvenate expired shrimp farms, and encourage women's livelihoods (Forests 2012). It is difficult to fully quantify the benefits of mangroves to fisheries, and this also applies to Tanakeke island, as estimated values of mangrove areas are just rough estimates (Forests 2012). Fisheries themselves are usually classified by the catch, not the ecosystems they rely on so understanding the full benefits of mangroves on fish is difficult to encompass at large scale due to the multi-dimensionality of benefits and the lack of coherent data, often leading to the undervaluation of mangroves (Seary et al. 2021).

As many studies have found that the majority of tropical fish species benefit from mangroves at some point in their life cycle, we think that our lack of statistical relationships between sites could be due to sampling design. Sites were selected based on habitat type, but the actual placement of the URUVs was influenced by other factors such as explicit permission from the landowner and the sites needed to be accessible from the boat and have clear video coverage of the area (i.e.~extremely dense mangrove forests that couldn't be accessed or too dark for the video footage). Further, even in destroyed sites, cameras had to be placed near vegetation in order to avoid boat and foot traffic that could influence the fish behavior and location while filming. Due to funding and resource availability, we were only able to collect data for 8 months, whereas a full year of data could paint a more robust picture of fish behavior and preference.

This study provides a knowledge base for the fish diversity in mangrove forests of Tanakeke Island, Indonesia. Since mangrove restoration is a critical undertaking, especially in Indonesia, this study serves as baseline understanding of fishery and mangrove function on Tanakeke. Long-term monitoring of restored mangroves has helped quantify the long-term economic benefits and environmental services of mangroves (Wirabuana et al. 2025). Conducting long-term surveys of fish assemblages in this ear could help better quantify the lasting impacts of restoration efforts.
